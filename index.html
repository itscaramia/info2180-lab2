The Spoiled Prince & The Traitor King

December 11, 1936 by History Department

Edward VIII grew up in immense royal privilege, developing into a notoriously spoiled, selfish, and entitled young man who despised his official duties. He hid immense private wealth while lying to his family about his finances, obsessed over maintaining a lavish lifestyle, and routinely showered his American mistress, Wallis Simpson, with wildly expensive jewelry. When his obsession forced a constitutional showdown, he casually walked away from the British empire, abdicating the throne in 1936 purely to indulge his personal desires.

The scandal worsened following his abdication when the newly titled Duke of Windsor began actively consorting with fascists, famously touring Nazi Germany in 1937 to shake hands with Adolf Hitler. Damning intelligence files later exposed his deep Nazi sympathies, revealing that Edward actively leaked state secrets, believed a German victory was inevitable, and even schemed with the Third Reich to be reinstated as a puppet King while London was being bombed.

Behind closed doors, the royal family knew the two brothers by their nicknames, David and Bertie. As the eldest sons of King George V and Queen Mary of Teck, David was the golden heir while Bertie was simply the reserve. David blossomed into a confident, highly photographed playboy who loved the nightlife, earning him a reputation among the British public as a modern "Prince Charming." Conversely, Bertie struggled with intense shyness and a severe speech impediment that eroded his self-esteem, leading others to falsely write him off as dull. Despite their contrasting personalities, the brothers shared a tight-knit bond during childhood due to their father's incredibly harsh upbringing.

This brotherly bond fractured in the early 1930s when David fell head-over-heels for Wallis Simpson. She quickly gained total control over him, bringing her own scandalous past, as a twice-divorced American socialite and dangerous, sympathetic fascist viewpoints into the royal circle. David began lying to his father to protect his relationship with Wallis, which deeply fractured the family. Bertie grew increasingly terrified; he knew that if his brother refused to settle down, the massive weight of the crown would collapse onto his own shoulders a destiny he desperately wanted to avoid due to his severe anxiety.

As King George V grew older, his relationship with Bertie strengthened, leading him to praise Bertie’s inner strength while bleakly predicting that David would ruin both himself and the empire within a year of taking the throne. When the King passed away in 1936, David ascended as Edward VIII, and the government’s worst fears came true. The new King proved to be stubborn, disorganized, and highly opinionated. He regularly left highly classified state papers scattered around his residence unsigned and stained with wine, and he routinely canceled vital public engagements at the last minute, forcing his stressed siblings to bail him out.

The situation exploded in late 1936 when Wallis finalized her second divorce and Edward insisted on marrying her. Because the Church of England forbade the monarch from marrying a divorcee, Prime Minister Baldwin flatly refused the match. The ensuing political chaos threw Bertie into a spiral of panic attacks and brought back his severe stutter. Even a series of desperate letters from Bertie's wife, Elizabeth, begging the King to reconsider, did nothing to sway him. Tensions peaked during a gathering at Balmoral, where Wallis breached royal protocol by acting as the hostess and openly mocking Elizabeth, permanently destroying the brothers' relationship. Isolated, exhausted, and facing a strict ultimatum from parliament regarding his Nazi sympathies and marital choices, David retreated to Fort Belvedere in December 1936 to sign away his crown.

George VI: Duty Amidst the Storm

September 03, 1939 by History Department

The sudden abdication forcefully pushed Albert onto the throne as King George VI—a destiny he completely dreaded, felt totally unprepared for, and initially wept over in private despair. While his greedy older brother secured an immense personal fortune, the new King was hit with immediate emotional and financial strain, forced to buy back the private crown estates out of his personal pocket. To manage his dangerous Nazi connections, the British government officially banished the disgraced Edward and Wallis to the Bahamas, assigning him a distant governorship to keep him isolated from the war effort.

While Edward lived in comfortable luxury overseas, King George VI stayed behind in London, directly enduring the heavy stress and physical terror of World War II. Refusing to abandon his people even when Buckingham Palace was hit by Nazi bombs, the King worked relentlessly, though the immense stress took an agonizing toll on his already fragile health. By the time the war ended, his body was shattered. He developed severe cardiovascular illness and lung cancer. His skin grew gaunt, and he hid severe, chronic physical pain from the eyes of a adoring public.

The ultimate heartbreak came on January 31, 1952, when his eldest daughter, Princess Elizabeth, had to step in to take his place on an exhausting tour of the Commonwealth because he was physically too weak to travel. Defying his medical doctors' strict advice to stay in bed, the visibly frail King insisted on standing out in the bitter winter chill at London Airport to personally wave his final goodbye. On the tarmac, as the BOAC airliner taxied away, the King stood completely motionless, hat in hand. He refused to leave the freezing airport tarmac, keeping his eyes locked onto the sky until her airplane was completely out of view. It was as if he intimately knew his time was spent and that it was the final time he would ever witness his beloved daughter alive. A mere six days later, King George VI passed away peacefully in his sleep at Sandringham House, leaving 25 year old Elizabeth to return home as Queen.
